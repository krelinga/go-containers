# Sorted slice vs B-tree as a sorted map's backing

From `bench.txt`, generated by `./run.sh` (`-count=10 -benchtime=200ms`).
Provenance is in that file's header. These ran on a **QEMU guest**; the slice
side is memmove-bound, so if anything its insert cost is *overstated* here and
the real crossover sits a little higher. **Ratios and the shape of the curves
are the durable result.**

B-tree side is `github.com/google/btree` (degree 32), not a hand-written tree.

## The one thing the slice loses: random-order insertion

ns per insert, building n entries in random key order:

| n | sorted slice | B-tree | winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 16.1 | 34.0 | slice, 2.1x |
| 64 | 48.4 | 62.4 | slice, 1.3x |
| **512** | **126.8** | **72.3** | **btree, 1.8x** |
| 4 096 | 672.8 | 110.5 | btree, 6.1x |
| 16 384 | 2 475.4 | 143.8 | btree, 17x |
| 65 536 | 9 418.7 | 160.6 | btree, 59x |

**The crossover is between n=64 and n=512** — lower than the "hundreds to low
thousands" rule of thumb. Above it the slice degrades quadratically while the
tree stays near-flat.

## Insertion *order* matters more than size

ns per insert, keys arriving already ascending:

| n | sorted slice | B-tree | winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 15.5 | 32.9 | slice, 2.1x |
| 512 | 15.6 | 33.8 | slice, 2.2x |
| 4 096 | 18.7 | 42.7 | slice, 2.3x |
| 65 536 | 24.1 | 56.1 | slice, 2.3x |

**The slice wins at every size and stays flat**, because an in-order insert is
an append — no memmove at all. Loading pre-sorted data, the common bulk case,
never touches the quadratic path.

## The slice wins every read

**Lookup** (ns/op): slice faster at every size — 6.1 vs 7.4 at n=8, 22.6 vs 33.4
at n=262 144. Roughly 1.4x throughout.

**Full ordered iteration** (ns/op): slice ~8x faster at every size — 59 802 vs
528 935 at n=262 144. Sequential memory versus pointer chasing.

**Range scan over 1% of keys**, both sides consuming every matched entry:

| n | sorted slice | B-tree |
|---|---|---|
| 512 | 28.8 | 34.2 |
| 4 096 | 42.6 | 152.5 |
| 65 536 | 208.2 | 2 070.2 |
| 262 144 | 645.1 | 8 417.6 |

An earlier version of this benchmark compared a slice *reslice* against a full
tree walk and reported 186x. That was measuring different work. Corrected, the
slice still wins ~13x at the largest size, on locality alone.

**Locating range bounds without consuming** is the one place the structures
differ in kind: a slice returns a sub-slice in O(log n) — 49 ns at n=262 144,
independent of how many entries the range holds. A tree has no equivalent.

## Conclusions

Durable:

1. A sorted slice loses to a B-tree **only** on random-order insertion, and only
   above roughly a hundred entries.
2. Insertion *order* dominates insertion *count*: in-order building is an append
   and beats the tree at every size measured.
3. The slice wins lookup, ordered iteration, and range scans at every size —
   iteration by ~8x, range scans by ~13x at scale.
4. Only a slice can hand back a range in O(log n) without walking it.

Perishable: every absolute number above.

**A sorted slice is the right backing unless the workload is random-order
insertion into a large map.** That limit is specific enough to document rather
than design around.
